JELIX: jellyfish-inspired underwater propulsion

A propeller-free propulsion mechanism for a small underwater vehicle. A cam–follower drive with spring return lifts four ribs slowly, then snaps them back to push out a jet of water, copying the pulse of the fried-egg jellyfish (Cotylorhiza tuberculata).

Problem

Jellyfish move by pulsing: they contract their bell to push out a jet of water, then relax. I wanted to know whether a simple mechanical linkage could reproduce that pulse well enough to propel a small underwater vehicle, as an alternative to a propeller.

Design targets

Envelope≈ 20 cm diameter, 20–25 cm long
ActuationOne motor, low voltage, no smart materials
MotionSlow expansion, fast contraction, repeating
Planned material6061 aluminum, for corrosion resistance in seawater
Not yet consideredDetailed manufacturing and sealing

How it works

The vehicle has an upper buoyancy segment and a lower propulsion segment. A motor turns a cam through a gear train. Each of the four ribs is a pivoted follower riding on the cam.

  • Slow rise (recovery stroke): the cam gently lifts the ribs and compresses the springs. Because the ribs move slowly, they push little water, so this stroke costs little reverse thrust.
  • Sudden drop (power stroke): the cam profile drops away, and the springs snap the ribs back fast. Thrust is the water's change in momentum divided by the time it takes, so the same stroke done faster pushes much harder, and water resistance also rises steeply with speed.
  • Net result: each cycle gives an upward push. To sink, the vehicle stops pulsing and lets buoyancy and gravity take over, so depth is controlled by how much it pulses.

Key decisions

1 · Mechanical cam instead of smart materials

OptionsShape-memory alloy or other smart-material "bell" · motor-driven cam and follower
ChoseCam and follower
WhyOne motor at low voltage, simple electronics, easy to build and repair, and the motion is repeatable and tunable by reshaping the cam

2 · Slow rise, sudden drop

GoalGet net thrust in one direction from a back-and-forth motion
ChoseA cam profile with a long, gradual rise and a short, steep drop
WhyThrust grows as stroke time shrinks. A slow return pushes little water backward; a fast snap pushes a lot of water forward, so the cycle nets out upward

3 · Profile defined every 10° by hand

MethodIn CATIA V5, I set the follower's distance from the cam center at every 10° of rotation, then joined the points with a smooth curve
Why 10°The goal was the overall shape (slow rise, sudden drop), not fine detail. Finer steps would have taken much longer without changing the motion that mattered

4 · 5 rpm input

SituationThe motor and gear ratio weren't chosen yet, so the real input speed was unknown
ChoseA fixed 5 rpm for simulation
WhyIt gave a clear, repeatable cycle (12 s) to compare cam versions against each other. It's a placeholder, not a design speed

Simulation

  • Exported the cam geometry from CATIA V5 to MSC ADAMS and ran a multibody simulation at 5 rpm over 24 s (two full cycles).
  • Exported the rib tip's displacement, velocity and acceleration from ADAMS to Excel and built all the plots there.
  • The raw velocity and acceleration were noisy. With my mentor's guidance, I applied a moving average, keeping the window small enough that it didn't wipe out the real motion.
Max X displacement (rib tip)≈ 65 mm
Max Y displacement (rib tip)≈ 80 mm
Angular stroke0° – 40°
Cycle period≈ 12 s (5 rpm input)

Free-body force model

I modeled the net force normal to each rib and split it into horizontal (Fx) and vertical (Fy) components across the stroke. Fy is the part that produces thrust, so the rib angle during the fast drop decides how much of each stroke becomes upward push.

What the simulation changed

Based on the first simulation runs, I changed three things:

Drop position on the camMoved from about 300° to about 270° of rotation
Maximum displacementAdjusted slightly to tune the rib stroke
Follower shapeReshaped so it stays in contact with the cam through the whole rotation, including the sudden drop

What I learned

  • Gravity was standing in for the springs. The ribs fell back under gravity in the simulation, which caused the noise in the data and is much gentler than what happens in water, where resistance slows the ribs down.
  • The fix I planned: a piston-assisted spring to snap the ribs back faster against water resistance.
  • Filtering is a judgment call. Smooth too much and you erase the motion you're trying to measure.
Status: design and simulation study. No physical prototype has been built, and no thrust has been measured.

Next steps

  • Model the springs (and the planned piston) instead of relying on gravity.
  • Add hydrodynamics (CFD, or a simple jet-impulse estimate) to predict thrust per stroke.
  • Choose a real motor and gear ratio, then rerun at the true input speed.
  • Print a one-rib benchtop rig and compare its measured motion against the ADAMS results.
